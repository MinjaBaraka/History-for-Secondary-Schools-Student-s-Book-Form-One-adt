Shifting cultivation involved livestock keeping as well. Shifting cultivators kept animals such as cattle, sheep, and goats. These animals provided them with milk, meat, blood, manure, and hides. In East Africa, such societies lived in central, southern, and western Tanzania. They also lived in most parts of central and north-eastern Kenya. The presence of tsetse flies infected both people and animals with trypanosomiasis (sleeping sickness). In West Africa, Mandinka and Bambara were among the societies that practised shifting cultivation.

Shifting cultivation had the major disadvantage of intensive labour power because it needed much labour to clear the vegetation before planting crops.

Permanent crop cultivation

This was a form of farming where people cultivated the same land for a long period without moving to another area. Pre-colonial African societies in the eastern, western, southern, central and northern parts of the continent practised permanent crop cultivation. In Central Africa, the Shona and Bemba were among the ethnic groups that practised permanent crop cultivation. The crops grown included millet, sorghum and beans. In West Africa, some of the ethnic groups that participated in permanent crop cultivation were the Ashanti, Yoruba, Akan and the Edo. Some of the crops grown included millet, yams, cassava, groundnuts, bambara nuts and palm oil.

In addition, permanent crop cultivation was also practised in the central and southern regions in the pre-colonial Africa. The Shona, Bemba, Sotho and Zulu were among the ethnic groups that practised permanent crop cultivation. The crops grown in these regions are maize, sorghum, millet and beans. In Northern Africa, the Berbers cultivated crops such as wheat, barley and olives. However, it is important to note that societies developed a unique agricultural system in a particular region, depending on the available resources, climatic condition and traditional beliefs.

Advantages of permanent crop cultivation

The following were the advantages of permanent crop cultivation. It led to an increase in agricultural production. Also, it stimulated population growth, since there was adequate food. Moreover, it stimulated the exchange of goods or trade the long-distance trade routes in Eastern Africa and West Africa show that exchange of agricultural and mineral goods took place.
