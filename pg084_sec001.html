Education system in pre-colonial African societies

Pre-colonial education is defined as a life-long process of transmitting and imparting knowledge, skills, values, norms and experiences from one generation to another in a given society. Pre-colonial education was delivered through active participation and observation. Each clan had ways of transmitting knowledge, skills, values and cultural norms, including taboos, to its young generation. Each adult participated in transmitting knowledge. Youth training depended on the experience of the trainer in a specific field. For example, the knowledge of midwifery could be imparted to some women, who would later train young girls to assist each other during pregnancy. They were also taught how to become acceptable wives and mothers.

Likewise, adults taught young men and women parental and marital responsibilities. For example, girls were taught a wide range of household and agricultural duties at an early age, including cooking, cultivation and attending children. Boys were taught to be responsible husbands and fathers. They also herded cows and looked after goats and other livestock. Children helped with minor household tasks and performed light duties for relatives.

Taboos were taught to younger generations to instill proper morals and protect natural resources like water, forest and land. For example, children in some societies were prohibited from going into the forest alone, claiming that they would meet with dangerous creatures that would harm them. Young generations inherited knowledge, values and wisdom from their parents and relatives, especially those related to the prosperity of society.

Aims of pre-colonial education

The pre-colonial education aimed at imparting knowledge, skills and values to young members of the society through word of mouth and direct involvement in activities like fishing, carpentry and painting. In most cases, it was learning by doing. It also imparted social values and transmitted good morals and norms to younger generations to prepare them for their future careers. For example, the youth were taught to do various economic activities such as fishing, farming and hunting. This went hand in hand with equipping them with the basic knowledge and skills related to particular economic activities. In that way, pre-colonial education developed a sense of belonging in learners and encouraged them to participate actively in performing family responsibilities.

The pre-colonial education was intended to develop children’s character, physical strength and intellectual abilities. Children were taught practical skills and self-dependence.
