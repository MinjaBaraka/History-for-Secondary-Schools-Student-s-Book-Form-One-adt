Furthermore, pastoralists required safe places for both people and their livestock. Examples of pastoralists in pre-colonial Africa were the Berbers and Tuaregs of North Africa, the Fulani and Hausa of West Africa, the Somali of North-Eastern Africa, the Karamojong of Uganda, the Maasai of Tanzania and Kenya, and the Khoikhoi of Southern Africa.

Division of labour in pastoral societies

Members of pastoral societies fulfilled various responsibilities at the family and community levels. However, most responsibilities were assigned according to age-sets and gender. Therefore, men, women, children, youth and elders carried out different responsibilities.

Duties and responsibilities of various groups

Women and children: Women stayed and worked at home. They cooked food, milked cows, grazed calves, and cared for the children. Children assisted women in fulfilling such duties.

Youth: The responsibilities of the youth were distributed according to their age groups. The youth aged 9 to 18 years were involved in livestock grazing during the afternoon and assisting their mothers in household duties when they returned home. The youth aged about 15 to 25 consisted of the middle-aged youths, who among the Maasai, were called the Moran. The Moran had six responsibilities, namely defending the community from enemies; protecting livestock against thieves and wild animals; raiding herds of neighbouring communities to obtain more livestock; driving cattle to new grazing land; collecting tribute from traders who passed on the Maasai territory; and surveying their neighbouring areas for pastures and sources of water.

Elders: This age group comprised of the elderly men in society. This group consisted of elders aged 40 and above. They were the senior age group, hence they were respected by other members of the society. The elders were also owners of cattle in their respective households. The most senior member of the elderly group was called laibon. He commanded special respect as the topmost cultural, political and religious authority in the community. The laibon also presided over major cultural and religious ceremonies. He was seen as the link between his people and the gods. The head of a council of laibons among the Maasai was known as Laigwanan. He was chosen from among the laibons representing different Maasai communities.
