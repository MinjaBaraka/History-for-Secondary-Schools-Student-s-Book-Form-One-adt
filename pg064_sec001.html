These communities were purposely located near water sources and grazing areas. Livestock in pre-colonial African societies remained important resources, providing not only food but also materials for clothing and shelter. Some of the societies that practised sedentary pastoralism were Khoikhoi and the San in the southern part of Africa. In the savannah regions of East Africa, the Maasai and Samburu practised sedentary pastoralism. In West Africa, some groups of the Fulani were nomadic; others adopted a settled lifestyle that included pastoral activities. Some animals kept were cattle, goats, sheep, camels and donkeys. Both nomadic and sedentary pastoralists were able to identify various herbs to treaty their diseased animals.

Mixed farming

Some societies combined animal keeping and crop cultivation. This type of agriculture is known as mixed farming. It was practised in areas with heavy rainfall as well as in grasslands with seasonal rainfall and woodland savannah. It was practised by people such as the Gogo, Nyaturu, Nyiramba, Sukuma, Nyamwezi, Hutu, Ankole, Pare and Kimbu. These people grew both permanent and seasonal crops such as bananas, yams, beans, and cereals. They also kept livestock such as cattle, sheep, goats and donkeys. The long-horned Ankole types of cattle were kept in the interlacustrine region. The short-horned cattle known as zebu were kept in the drier woodland savannah and wooded steppes. Other societies that practised mixed farming in pre-colonial Africa were Rwozi in Central Africa. They applied irrigation and terracing methods. In West Africa the Hausa, Yoruba, Ewe, Ashanti, Nupe and Igbo practised mixed farming. These societies cultivated variety of cash crops and raised livestock. They grew crops such as millet, sorghum, yams, maize, and raised livestock like cattle, sheep and goats.
