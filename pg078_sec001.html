In pre-colonial African societies, the development of social, political and educational systems was not similar in all parts of the continent.

It varied from one society to another due to factors like geographical location and technological development of a particular society.

Some societies practised non-centralised political systems and others had reached centralised political systems.

The two political systems differed on how power was shared among the society members and the nature of social relations that existed.

Non-centralised political system

Non-centralised or decentralised social and political systems were a form of organisation with no central authority.

Some of the common characteristics of non-centralised political system included:

They did not have a central ruler known as a king; their political leaders were mainly clan heads, assisted by councils of elders; political power and authority were shared among the council of elders and clan heads.

There were two forms of non-centralised social and political systems in pre-colonial Africa, namely clan or kinship organisation and the age-set system.

Clan or kinship organisation

A kinship or clan organisation is a type of social and political organisation involving closely related families with the same or common ancestor.

This was the most common form of kinship practised worldwide, including pre-colonial Africa.

A clan consisted of several families, which are closely related together.

Such families consisted of the husband, wife or wives, children, grandparents and extended relatives.

The family was the basic unit of production.

Some examples of pre-colonial African societies that practised clan or kinship organisation were the Igbo of West Africa, Sukuma of Tanzania and the Kikuyu of Kenya.

There were two main types of kinship in pre-colonial Africa.

These were patrilineal and matrilineal.

These groups were created for various reasons but served the same goals.
